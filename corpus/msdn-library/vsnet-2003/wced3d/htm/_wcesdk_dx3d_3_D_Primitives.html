3-D Primitives

A 3-D primitive is a collection of vertices that form a single 3-D entity. The simplest primitive is a collection of points in a 3-D coordinate system, which is called a point list.

Often, 3-D primitives are polygons. A polygon is a closed 3-D figure delineated by at least three vertices. From a theoretical perspective, polygons are always planar. In practice, polygons may deviate from their planar ideal because of numerical precision and rounding errors. The simplest polygon is a triangle. Microsoft® Direct3D® uses triangles with shared vertices to compose most of its polygons because all three vertices in a triangle are guaranteed to be coplanar. Rendering nonplanar vertices is inefficient and prone to error in most clipping and rasterization algorithms because the represented surface is not guaranteed to be convex. You can combine triangles to form large, complex polygons and meshes.

The following illustration shows a cube. Two triangles form each face of the cube and all adjacent triangles share vertices. The entire set of triangles forms one seamless cubic primitive. If the adjacent triangles did not share vertices and instead used coincident points, it would be possible for cracks or seems to appear between triangles. You can apply textures and materials to the surfaces of primitives to make them appear to be a single solid form. For more information, see Materials and Textures.

You can also use triangles to create primitives whose surfaces appear to be smooth curves. The following illustration shows how a sphere can be simulated with triangles. After a material is applied, the sphere looks curved when it is rendered. This is especially true if you use Gouraud shading when the surface is lit and shaded..
